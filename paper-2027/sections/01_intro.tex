\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rotary position embedding (RoPE) assigns a different rotation frequency to each
pair of query and key coordinates \citep{su2024roformer}. In the usual
parameterization, $\omega_k=b^{-\phi_k}$, a single base $b$ and an equally spaced
exponent sequence $\phi_k$ determine the entire table. Changing the base is a
well-established way to adjust the range of positional scales
\citep{liu2024scaling,xu2024base,oka2026fmrope}. The exponents make another
choice: how the available rotary pairs are distributed within that range.
What changes when this distribution is designed explicitly?

This question matters because each attention head has finitely many rotary
pairs. Over a bounded context, several frequencies can produce almost the same
positional response. For example, in a standard $64$-pair table with base
$500{,}000$, the $23$ pairs that rotate by at most one radian over $4$K tokens
have a block-whitened R\'enyi-$2$ effective rank of approximately two under uniform separations. Changing their
spacing changes the basis available to the model while preserving the rotation
operator. We study this allocation through its exponents, from their geometry
to constructions that can be evaluated in language models.

Our first step separates exponent shape from frequency range. Figure~\ref{fig:spectral-budget-overview}
compares two tables with identical endpoints and pair count. One uses equally
spaced exponents; the other redistributes the interiors using a Cosh density.
Across three paired training seeds, the reallocation improves all three tested
extrapolation lengths. The paired design exposes the contribution of exponent
shape to length generalization.

We develop the allocation in two stages. First, we analyze the full sine--cosine
subspace associated with each frequency. The analysis identifies the shared
slow-frequency limit and motivates a convex surrogate variational problem for distributing
exponents. Its solution yields \evq{}, an analytic inverse-CDF construction
that changes only the frequency table. We evaluate it through fixed-range
controls, language-model training, and matched adaptation. With $16$ rotary
pairs in a $432$M MLA model, it reduces $16$K perplexity by $31.1\%$. Under
matched Llama-$3$-$8$B adaptation, $32$K perplexity falls from $991.5$ to
$127.9$, with the full original-window and extrapolation curves reported together.

Second, we study how exponents can be adjusted in an already trained model.
Such a model has learned to use its original table, so the adjustment is defined
relative to that table. Existing methods provide useful constructions:
YaRN blends frequencies across bands, MrRoPE distributes a cumulative scale
through mixed-radix factors, and learned-frequency methods optimize the entries
\citep{peng2024yarn,tian2026mrrope,karypis2026lerope,wang2026adarope}.
We express these operations as exponent displacements and use matched experiments
to compare their shapes. This stage includes a single static profile that
improves Qwen's RULER score over YaRN at $64$K while retaining its $32$K score, and a
boundary-matched redistribution with gains on five OLMo natural-QA tasks.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We (i) formulate exponent allocation independently of sampled range and analyze
its phase-invariant positional geometry; (ii) derive a closed-form allocation
and establish its effects through paired controls, training, and adaptation;
and (iii) derive a boundary-matched adjustment for frozen models and evaluate
structured displacement profiles through matched retrieval and natural-QA tests.
The common object is the exponent distribution: the two stages examine how to
construct it before learning and how to adjust it after weights have been learned.
