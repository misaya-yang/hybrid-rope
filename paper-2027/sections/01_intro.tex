\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Rotary position embeddings (RoPE) assign a frequency to each two-dimensional query--key coordinate pair \citep{su2024roformer}. Its standard geometric table is parameterized by a base, controlling sampled positional scales \citep{liu2024scaling,xu2024base}. A finite table, however, makes another choice: how its rotary pairs occupy that range \citep{oka2026fmrope,tian2026mrrope}. What remains necessary is to distinguish the causal effect of interior allocation from range selection, from the learning process, and from the compatibility of an installed table with mature weights.

Three questions organize the study. What changes in the positional basis when interior exponents are redistributed? Does this redistribution affect learned behavior when the frequency range is held strictly fixed? And how does an existing checkpoint respond to adjustments of its native exponents? These questions connect geometric analysis, controlled interventions, and practical table design.

Figure~\ref{fig:spectral-budget-overview} shows the basic intervention: decompose the log-frequency table into endpoints $(a,R)$ and normalized interior exponents $z$. In paired $151.9$M training runs ($L_{\mathrm{train}}=256, K=32$), holding endpoints identical while changing only the $30$ interior exponents lowers extrapolation NLL across all three seeds. However, retargeting the runtime range reverses this ordering, and frozen weights strongly prefer runtime tables derived from their training allocation. Together, these controls isolate interior allocation as an independent design variable while establishing its action boundaries with range and learned representations.

The positional geometry provides a way to analyze these distributions. Each rotary pair supplies an entire sine--cosine subspace. Canonical correlations compare these subspaces without privileging a content phase, exposing the common low-frequency limit $\operatorname{span}\{1,\Delta\}$. Thus many slow pairs supply few distinct positional directions over a bounded interval. The learned content coefficients determine how the model uses those directions.

We evaluate concrete allocation designs in two regimes. Before or during learning, a convex design criterion produces an analytic Cosh family whose quantiles give a fixed table, evaluated in scarce rotary channels, continued training, and matched $8$B adaptation. For frozen checkpoints, we express modifications as displacements from native exponents. A static Qwen configuration improves $64$K RULER-13 by $6.09$ points over YaRN, and a boundary-matched adjustment raises OLMo mean token F1 from $21.62\%$ to $25.44\%$ across five natural-QA tasks ($631$ untruncated prompts). Checkpoint preferences differ across lengths, making learned compatibility central to allocation design.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
We (i) isolate interior exponent allocation from sampled range using paired training and frozen-model interventions, showing behavioral effects that cannot be explained by scalar range changes alone; (ii) characterize full sine--cosine subspace geometry, establish their shared slow-frequency limit, and delimit static metrics via non-identifiability and compatibility boundaries; and (iii) evaluate explicit allocation designs (analytic Cosh and boundary-matched) under matched learning and static deployment with complete-generation and natural-QA endpoints.
