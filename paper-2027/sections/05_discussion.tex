\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Designed interior allocations produce strong capability changes in fully frozen
checkpoints, remain actionable under matched low-rank adaptation, and alter
outcomes through training-time weight--table co-adaptation. These three
intervention stages establish $z$ as a controllable RoPE design coordinate.
Exact full-subspace geometry explains how allocations change the available
positional basis; the weights-by-table crossing and transplant obstruction
explain why the intervention stage matters.

Support and allocation are complementary, interacting coordinates. The
fixed-support controls hold $K$, both endpoints, and the RoPE operator constant
to identify $z$ alone, while target-aware support retargeting reverses the tested
ordering. A realised table therefore reflects both sampled support $(a,R)$ and
the interior allocation $z$ of its fixed $K$ pairs; frequency transport may
change both at once.

Across the autoregressive text protocols that evaluate both the original
training or Native window and longer positions, the comparisons reveal a
within-protocol crossover: the baseline is stronger near the original window,
while the reallocated table overtakes it beyond that window. This recurring
behavioural signature of finite-budget reallocation directly motivates the
Native/long session route. The video DiT shows another regime: both
training-frame and extrapolated-frame MSE improve ($-21\%$ on training frames
and $-35\%$ on far extrapolated frames), and its pre-specified diagnostic
connects the gain to in-window phase coverage. Together with LeRoPE's learned
in-window allocations, these results make a single zero-training allocation
that itself improves Native-window and long-range capability---rather than
preserving the former through routing---the natural next method direction.

The practical consequence is that a finite RoPE table should be specified by
both its sampled support and its interior allocation. A shared base, endpoint,
or pair count does not by itself define a matched finite table. Treating $z$ as
an explicit design object makes allocation measurable and controllable across
training, adaptation, and deployment.
