\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

A finite RoPE table exposes two choices: support $(a,R)$ and normalised
allocation $z$. Scalar ``effective base'', median wavelength, and cycle counts
summarise $z$, but none reproduces a non-geometric table whose endpoints are
fixed. The exact-range intervention identifies this degree of freedom
directly. Theorem~\ref{thm:budget} then accounts for redundancy in the resulting
sin/cos basis through its Rényi-$2$ effective rank. In contrast, trained value
depends on how Q/K weights use that basis: a table can have higher static rank
and still be wrong for weights trained in another coordinate system.

\paragraph{Why the axis matters.}
A scalar-base sweep explores only the geometric path
$z_k=k/(K{-}1)$; every non-geometric allocation lies outside that path, even
when the sampled endpoints are unchanged. This is more than a change of
coordinates on paper. The fixed-support intervention changes what the model
learns, and the effect becomes consequential when a long range must be represented
by few rotary pairs. The $432$M MLA result makes that regime concrete: with
$K{=}16$, changing the allocation leaves the training-length PPL nearly fixed
but lowers $16$K PPL in every seed. Learned-frequency methods can also search
beyond the geometric path, but they combine the table choice with an additional
optimization problem. A closed-form allocation instead makes the design
decision explicit, reproducible, and testable without learned positional
parameters.

The scale experiments answer a different practical question: whether the
effective-context shift survives realistic ways of obtaining a model. It
appears after full-parameter continuation from a shared checkpoint at $750$M,
in a from-initialisation trajectory at $1.485$B, and after low-rank adaptation
of pretrained models. Their common pattern is not uniform dominance but a
redistribution of modelling quality from the training window toward longer
contexts. This is precisely the trade a spectral budget is meant to expose.

This is why allocation belongs in training or adaptation. The $50$M
table-by-weights crossing shows that the coefficients co-adapt to their table,
and Theorem~\ref{thm:obstruction} gives the exact obstruction: fixed invertible
Q/K maps cannot preserve all relative-position logits when the frequency
multisets differ. Post-hoc replacement and training with a table are therefore
different interventions. The mature-model results show that low-rank
adaptation can still exploit a new allocation without changing the operator.

Allocation is also different from range transport. The former chooses which
finite channels the model learns to use; the latter redeploys a realised
spectrum at a target length. The same \rs{} transform has different leverage
on Geo and \evq{} substrates, and the target-retargeted diagnostic in
App.~\ref{sec:identification-details} shows that the two choices interact.
Holding support fixed identifies allocation; choosing them jointly remains a
deployment decision. LeRoPE provides complementary evidence from learning: a
table obtained in one run retains substantial value when frozen for a new
training run \citep{karypis2026lerope}. Here, the closed-form construction
removes the learned-table search while the fixed-support control isolates what
the table changes.

The broader result is simple. At finite $K$, interior frequency placement is
part of the training design. Support says which frequencies are available;
allocation says how the head spends them. Both should be chosen with the
deployment range in mind, but the allocation must be present while the model
learns how to use its positional basis.
