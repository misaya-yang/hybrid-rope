\subsection{Complete rotary pairs and positional overlap}
\label{sec:theory}
\label{sec:subspaces}
\label{sec:budget}

For a fixed content pair, one rotary channel contributes $C\cos(\omega\Delta)+D\sin(\omega\Delta)$ to an attention logit, where $\Delta$ is relative token separation. Both coefficients depend on content; its positional object is the full function space
\begin{equation}
V_\omega=\operatorname{span}\{\cos(\omega\Delta),\sin(\omega\Delta)\}.
\label{eq:subspace}
\end{equation}
Comparing only cosine curves would privilege one content phase. Instead, let $x_\omega(\Delta)=[\cos(\omega\Delta),\sin(\omega\Delta)]$ and define $S_\omega=\mathbb E[x_\omega^\top x_\omega]$, $H_{\omega\nu}=\mathbb E[x_\omega^\top x_\nu]$ under a declared separation measure. For nonsingular self-Grams, the singular values of $Q_{\omega\nu}=S_\omega^{-1/2}H_{\omega\nu}S_\nu^{-1/2}$ are the canonical correlations of the two spaces. Their mean square,
\begin{equation}
c_{\omega\nu}=\tfrac12\|Q_{\omega\nu}\|_F^2\in[0,1],
\label{eq:collision}
\end{equation}
measures shared positional directions and is invariant to invertible basis changes within either pair. Let $\Gamma$ be the Gram matrix of the concatenated block-whitened pairs, with diagonal blocks $I_2$. The average pairwise overlap $\bar c$ gives the exact R\'enyi-$2$ effective-rank identity
\begin{equation}
r_2(\Gamma)=\frac{(\operatorname{tr}\Gamma)^2}{\operatorname{tr}(\Gamma^2)}
=\frac{2K}{1+(K-1)\bar c}.
\label{eq:budget-identity}
\end{equation}
This connects pairwise overlap to the number of effective directions supplied by the entire positional design matrix: increasing overlap concentrates the same $2K$ coordinates into fewer directions.

\subsection{Finite-window concentration of slow directions}
\label{sec:slow-limit}

\begin{proposition}[Shared slow-frequency subspace]
\label{prop:collapse}
Under uniform measure on $[0,L]$, with $t=\Delta/L$, as $x=\omega L\to0$, $\cos(xt)\to1$ and $\sin(xt)/x\to t$, so $V_\omega$ converges to $\operatorname{span}\{1,\Delta\}$. For $\epsilon=\max\{|\omega L|,|\nu L|\}\to0$, squared chordal separation satisfies $2-\|Q_{\omega\nu}\|_F^2=\mathcal O(\epsilon^4)$.
\end{proposition}

Distinct slow frequencies therefore approach a shared two-dimensional positional space. On the standard grid $b=500{,}000$, $K=64$, $L=4096$, the $23$ pairs with $\omega L\le1$ occupy $46$ coordinates but have block-whitened rank $2.00013$. The finite-window calculation also reaches the core experiment: at $b=256$, $K=32$, $L=256$, the eight slowest pairs span $\omega L\in[1.19,4]$ and have rank $2.11$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:allocation-basis}). Their overlap is substantial even though every frequency has $\omega L>1$.

\caption{\textbf{Log-frequency spacing and positional directions are different objects.} Uniform separation measure on $[0,L]$. (a) Canonical overlap of full sine--cosine pairs on the actual $b=256$, $K=32$, $L=256$ identification grid. The eight slowest pairs share most of their positional space. (b) Effective rank as progressively faster pairs enter that slow subset: eight pairs give $16$ coordinates but $r_2=2.11$. Values are closed-form Gram calculations checked by independent quadrature.}

The geometry explains why allocating frequencies uniformly in log space need not spread positional directions uniformly over a finite window. It supplies a concrete object for design: where samples are placed changes which scales and directions are represented. Model behavior then depends on the content coefficients learned on this basis. The next interventions examine that learned use directly; complete geometric proofs and cross-length ordering examples appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:geometry-proofs}--\ref{sec:static-counterexamples}.
