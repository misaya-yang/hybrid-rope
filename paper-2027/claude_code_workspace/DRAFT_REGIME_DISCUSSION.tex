\paragraph{Two regimes of one allocation problem.}
The coordinate split $x_k = a + R z_k$ describes the supplied basis, but the
objective that allocation must serve changes once weights exist. Before or during
learning there is no compatible weight set to consult, so the only available signal
is the geometry of the supplied basis itself; the Cosh family is the closed-form
allocation that a stated convex criterion selects from that signal
(\S\ref{sec:construction}). After learning, the checkpoint has already assigned
roles to specific slots, and the allocation can only be moved relative to the
coordinates those weights were trained against; the native-relative profiles
of \S\ref{sec:mature-adjustments} are the corresponding construction. The two
regimes are therefore not alternative heuristics but the same design problem posed
with and without $M$.

\paragraph{The regime boundary is measured, not assumed.}
The weights-by-table crossings in Fig.~\ref{fig:weight-table-crossing} place the
boundary empirically. Two weight sets trained under different interior allocations
each perform best under the runtime table derived from their own training
allocation, and a table that is favourable as a supplied basis can be catastrophic
once installed: in the $50$M crossing, installing Cosh frequencies into
Geo-trained weights raises PPL to $76.20$, while the reverse swap raises it to
$23.05$. Symmetrically, the retargeting experiment shows the ordering of two
allocations reversing after the operating range is changed. An allocation is
therefore not a property of a table that can be evaluated before deciding where
it will be used.

\paragraph{Why static table search is structurally incomplete.}
A recurring programme seeks the best RoPE table by scoring candidate frequency
schedules with a static geometric functional---entropy, effective rank, turn count,
orbit structure, or a fixed profile---and then deploying the winner. Our results
give a reason to expect that programme to underdetermine the answer, and the reason
is not that the functionals are poorly chosen. By Theorem~\ref{thm:obstruction},
the only equivalence that a schedule admits, up to position-independent
reparameterization, is sign and permutation; permutation is precisely the
transformation that a trained model does not survive
(Theorem~\ref{thm:non-identifiability}). A schedule-only functional must therefore
be invariant to something, and the invariances the schedule offers are either the
fatal one or, as in the orbit-count and coordinate-distance witnesses, quantities
that the measurements here dissociate from behaviour. What the functional cannot
see is $M$: the roles that training assigned to individual slots. This is the sense
in which the design space has three coordinates rather than one, and it is why
ranking tables without the operating range and the compatible weight set is not
merely difficult but ill-posed.

\paragraph{What this does and does not establish.}
The identification result is strong where it is measured: paired $151.9$M training
at strictly fixed range changes only the $30$ interior exponents, and the
extrapolation ordering reverses under range retargeting in all three seeds. The
crossings are measured on two scales. We do not claim that no schedule-only
statistic can ever be useful, only that none of those tested is sufficient alone;
and the constructions here are witnesses that the space yields real gains, not
claims of a universal optimum.
